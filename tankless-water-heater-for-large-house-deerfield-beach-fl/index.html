Tankless Water Heater for a Large House in Deerfield Beach, FL

A large house can run on tankless hot water, but one unit is often not enough. Sizing starts with how many fixtures run at the same time, measured in gallons per minute, and how much the water must be heated. Big homes with several showers, a soaking tub and a laundry room often need two units, a larger unit or a different system.

The unit is only half the question. The gas line or electric panel feeding it has to carry the load, and that is where many large-house projects get complicated. This guide walks through each factor so you can plan before you shop.

By the Deerfield Beach Pro Plumbing teamPublished October 23, 2026Updated October 23, 2026

Size by flow, not by square footage

Square footage is a poor guide to tankless sizing. A 5,000 square foot house with two people needs less hot water at once than a smaller house with five people getting ready for school. What matters is peak demand, meaning the most hot water the household draws at the same moment.

Each fixture has a flow rate in gallons per minute. A shower, a kitchen faucet and a washing machine may all run together. Add their flows, and you have the number the heater must deliver while still reaching the temperature you want.

We walk through the fixtures with you, count what could run at once, and size from that. Our tankless water heater page explains the installation side. A large house also has long pipe runs, and the wait for hot water at a far bathroom is a plumbing question, not just a heater question.

Sizing too small is the common mistake. The unit then struggles when two showers and a dishwasher overlap, and the water turns lukewarm exactly when the house is busiest. Sizing too large wastes money and adds gas or electric demand you did not need, so the goal is a close fit with a little room to spare.

Gas line and electric panel limits

Tankless units use a lot of energy while they are running. A gas unit pulls far more gas at once than a tank heater does, and the gas line often has to be upsized to supply it. A larger-diameter line or a new meter connection may be part of the project, which affects both price and schedule.

Electric tankless units need heavy circuits, sometimes several. Large homes may not have spare panel capacity for even one, and two units can mean a panel upgrade. Before choosing electric, have the panel checked, because the electrical work can cost more than the heater itself.

Gas units also need correct venting and combustion air. Permit rules vary, so ask the City of Deerfield Beach building department what applies to your project. Work follows the Florida Building Code, Plumbing, and any gas piping upgrade should be handled by a properly qualified professional.

What sizing looks like in South Florida

Incoming water in South Florida tends to be warmer than in cold climates, so the temperature rise the heater has to deliver is often smaller. In general terms, that can help a tankless unit keep up. We still size from your actual fixtures and incoming temperature, not from a general rule.

There is no freeze risk in Deerfield Beach, which opens up placement options such as garage walls and outdoor mounting. Exterior units still face salt air near the coast, heavy summer rain and storm season, so clearances, drainage and protection matter. Many local homes sit on slabs, which means heaters often end up in garages, closets or utility rooms.

If you live in a condo or managed community, ask the association before planning venting or exterior work. We also serve nearby towns, including the area on our Coconut Creek page. You can find the full list on our service areas page.

When two tankless units or a hybrid makes sense

Two units often beat one oversized unit in a large home. They split the load, give you backup if one needs service and can sit near the bathrooms they serve, which shortens waits for hot water. The trade-off is the extra equipment, venting and supply work.

A heat pump hybrid or a small storage tank paired with a tankless unit is worth discussing for homes with very high peak demand. The tank covers short surges, and the tankless unit handles steady use. For a planned install, our water heater installation team can lay out the options and explain the trade-offs honestly.

Questions to settle before you buy

Run through this list with your plumber. The answers shape the whole project.

  • What is the peak demand in gallons per minute Count the fixtures that could run together on a busy morning.
  • Can the gas line or panel support the unit Ask for this to be checked before a unit is chosen.
  • Where will the unit sit Garage walls, utility rooms and exterior mounting each have different clearance and venting needs.
  • How long is the wait at the farthest bathroom A second unit or recirculation loop may fix it better than a bigger heater.
  • Who flushes and services it Tankless units need periodic flushing, and access matters when a technician has to reach the unit.
  • Does the community allow it Condo and HOA rules may require approval for gas work, venting or exterior placement.
