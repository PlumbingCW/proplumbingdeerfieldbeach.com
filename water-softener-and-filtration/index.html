Start by testing your tap, not by buying equipment

Treatment equipment only makes sense once you know what it is supposed to treat. A basic hardness test strip or a lab test can tell you how much mineral content your tap water carries, and a few minutes with a kit costs far less than guessing wrong. Test at a kitchen cold tap, and test again after the water has run for a minute.

You can also ask the City of Deerfield Beach for its annual water quality report. The city's drinking water comes from groundwater, drawn from the Biscayne and Floridan aquifers through 14 active production wells, and the treatment process includes lime softening, nanofiltration and reverse osmosis streams that are blended, pH adjusted and disinfected. The report we reviewed lists no hardness figure, so we do not guess at your number. Your own test result is what counts.

Bring the numbers and your complaints to us. If the results show little to treat, we will say so and save you the expense.

Softener or filter: two tools for two different jobs

People often use the words as if they mean the same thing. They do not, and mixing them up is the most common reason a system disappoints.

Water softener

  • Uses a resin tank and salt to swap out hardness minerals
  • Aimed at scale on fixtures, glass and heater elements
  • Needs salt refills and an occasional cleaning cycle
  • Sends a bit of wastewater to the drain during regeneration
  • Does not improve taste, smell or remove chloramine

Filtration or reverse osmosis

  • Uses carbon, sediment media or a membrane to treat taste, smell and particles
  • Aimed at drinking, cooking and general water quality
  • Needs filter or membrane changes on a schedule
  • Whole-house units protect every tap, RO units usually serve one sink
  • Does not remove scale-forming minerals the way a softener does

Some homes end up with both, a softener for scale and a carbon filter or an RO tap for drinking water. Others need only one. Your test results and your complaints decide.

Chloramine and the system you choose

The City of Deerfield Beach disinfects its water with chloramine, and the city switches to free chlorine for about three weeks each year. That matters because filters are not all equal. Standard carbon filters are often sold for chlorine taste, and chloramine is harder to remove, so a unit that handles one may do little for the other.

Before you buy any filter, ask whether it is rated for chloramine, and ask what flow rate and contact time that rating assumes. A filter that works on paper but strangles the flow at the kitchen sink is a poor trade. Carbon media also needs timely replacement, because an exhausted cartridge stops treating the water while still looking fine.

Reverse osmosis units use a membrane plus carbon stages and are a common pick for drinking water at one tap. They produce wastewater and need cartridge changes, and the membrane is not meant to feed the whole house.

Questions to ask before you sign for any system

A good installer welcomes questions. These keep the sales pitch honest.

  • What problem does this solve? The answer should match your test results and your complaint, not a general claim about all water.
  • Is it rated for chloramine? Ask for the specific rating on the product sheet, not a verbal yes.
  • What does it cost to run? Salt, filter cartridges, membranes and water used for flushing all add up each year.
  • Where will it go and how will it drain? Many local garages and utility closets are tight, and a softener needs a drain nearby plus a power outlet.
  • What happens to my warranty and my water heater? Ask how the manufacturer and installer treat service, and have our team explain how treated water may affect the heater.
  • Who services it later? A system without a plan for parts and repair becomes a bypass valve nobody uses.
  • Does my building allow it? Condo and HOA rules often govern what can be added to shared water lines.
