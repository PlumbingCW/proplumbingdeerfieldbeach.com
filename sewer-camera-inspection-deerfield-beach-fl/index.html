Sewer Camera Inspection in Deerfield Beach, FL: What It Shows and When to Book One

A sewer camera inspection sends a waterproof camera on a flexible rod through your drain line and records what it sees. It finds roots, cracks, offsets, belly sections and buildup, and it measures how far each problem sits from the entry point. The best times to book one are before buying a property, after repeated backups and before a remodel.

The video turns a guess into evidence. This guide explains how the process works, what the report includes and where the camera runs out of reach.

By the Deerfield Beach Pro Plumbing teamPublished October 23, 2026Updated October 23, 2026

On site with the camera

The camera head has a light and a lens, and it travels on a push rod that the technician feeds into the line through a cleanout. A monitor shows the live picture, and the system records the run. The distance counter tells us how far the camera has traveled, so each defect gets a location.

Locating tools finish the job. A small transmitter in the camera head sends a signal that a hand-held receiver can track from above ground. That tells us where the pipe runs, how deep it sits and where to mark the ground if a repair is needed.

Our sewer camera inspection service uses this method on residential and light commercial lines. We do not dig on the strength of a hunch. We look first, and then we talk about what, if anything, needs to happen.

Most of the visit is calm and unglamorous. The technician sets up at the cleanout, feeds the rod steadily and stops whenever something unusual appears on screen. Taking time at each suspect spot is what separates a useful recording from a quick pass down the pipe.

From the first call to the finished report

The process is short, and each step has a purpose.

  1. 1

    Find the access point

    We look for a cleanout outside the building. If there is none, we may use a roof vent, a removed toilet or another access.

  2. 2

    Clear the line if needed

    A line packed with grease or paper blocks the lens. If so, we clear it first so the camera can see the pipe walls.

  3. 3

    Run the camera and record

    The camera moves through the line while we watch the monitor and note each defect and its distance.

  4. 4

    Locate and mark

    Using locating tools, we trace the camera from above ground and mark any spot where a repair might be needed.

  5. 5

    Review the findings together

    You see the video, hear the options and receive the written summary.

When a camera inspection earns its cost

Not every plumbing problem needs a camera. These situations are where it helps most.

Buying a home or condo

A standard home inspection does not look inside the buried drain line. A camera can find a cracked or root-filled pipe before closing, when you still have room to negotiate.

Repeated backups

A line that clogs again and again usually has a reason. The camera shows whether it is roots, a belly, a bad joint or a collapsed section.

After a repair

A follow-up run proves the new work is sound and that nothing else is failing nearby.

Before a remodel or addition

If you are adding a bathroom or building over the line, you should know its condition and route first.

Slow drains with a sewer smell

A p-trap that will not hold water, a sewer odor or several slow drains can point to a venting or line problem that a camera helps narrow.

Reading the report: what the video shows

The footage shows the pipe material, its general condition and any defects. Roots appear as fine hair-like threads or thick masses entering at joints. A crack shows as a dark line, an offset joint as a step in the pipe wall and a belly as a section holding standing water.

A good report names each finding, gives its distance from the entry point and says how serious it is. It separates problems that need action from those you can monitor. Ask for a copy of the video, since it helps with insurance conversations, negotiations and any later repair.

If the camera finds damage, our sewer line repair page explains the repair options. The recording keeps everyone talking about the same pipe.

Keep in mind that a clean report is a snapshot, not a promise. Roots can grow back, and a pipe in good shape today can shift later, so a line with a history of trouble may be worth checking again after a repair or a few seasons of heavy use.

Buying a home or condo in Deerfield Beach

If you are buying, ask whether the seller will allow a sewer camera inspection during your due diligence period. A recording of the line can support a request for repair or a price adjustment. In a condo, ask the association which lines are yours and which are shared, since the camera only helps for the pipe you are responsible for.

Before you hire anyone, verify their state license through the Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation online services. Under Fla. Stat. 489.128, an unlicensed contractor cannot enforce its contract, which can leave you without recourse. It is a simple check that protects your money.

Many Deerfield Beach homes and condos are built on concrete slabs, so lines often run under floors and driveways. That makes a camera inspection even more useful before any digging. What a camera can show and what it cannot

A camera inspection is a strong tool with real limits. Knowing them avoids false confidence.

What it can show

  • Roots, cracks and offset joints inside the pipe
  • Belly sections that hold water
  • Buildup such as grease and scale
  • The distance and approximate depth of a defect
  • The path of the line when paired with locating tools

What it cannot do

  • See past a full blockage without clearing it first
  • Confirm leaks in pressurized water supply lines
  • Prove a pipe will never fail in the future
  • Reach lines with no workable access point
  • Replace a plumber's judgment about repair options

If you suspect a leak behind walls or under a slab, that is a different test. Our leak detection service uses acoustic listening, thermal imaging and pressure testing for supply lines.

How to get the most from an inspection

A few habits make the visit more useful and the report easier to trust.

  • Tell us the history Share past backups, repairs and which fixtures act up.
  • Point out the cleanout If you know where it is, show us. If not, we will find one.
  • Ask for the video A recording is better than a verbal summary.
  • Get findings in writing The report should list each defect and its distance.
  • Ask about priorities Not every finding needs action now, so ask what is urgent.
  • Verify the plumber Check any contractor's state license through DBPR before hiring.
