Water or heat is coming up through the floor

A pressurized line under concrete can leak for a long time, but it can also worsen quickly. Floors that feel warm, tile that lifts, or carpet that squishes underfoot mean water is already moving into the home.

Close the main shut-off

Turn off the main valve where the water line enters the building. In many local homes it is near the water heater, in the garage or on an exterior wall. Closing it stops the flow into the slab line right away.

Turn off the water heater supply

If hot water is escaping under the floor, shut the cold inlet valve on top of the tank and switch off the heater's power or gas. A heater that keeps heating while water drains out can be damaged.

Keep feet and power away from wet areas

Do not stand in standing water near outlets, appliances or the panel. If a breaker needs to be shut off and you cannot reach it dry, tell us when you call and we will plan around it.

Document before you clean

Photograph the floor, the stained baseboards and your water meter. Insurers usually ask for evidence of when and how the problem started.

In a condo, tell your property manager or association as soon as you can. A slab line can serve neighboring units, and building rules often decide who may open the floor.

Why slab leaks happen and why they are so common here

Many Deerfield Beach homes and condos are built on concrete slabs with few or no basements, so supply lines frequently run through or under the floor. Over time, those pipes face abrasion against the concrete, shifting soil, corrosion from the outside and constant pressure from the inside.

Hot water lines tend to fail first, because heat expands and contracts the pipe every time the heater cycles. Copper can also develop pinholes from chemical reaction with the surrounding soil or concrete. Ask the city for its annual water quality report if you want to know what is in your water, and test your tap if you suspect it plays a role.

Homes closer to the coast, around Main Beach and The Cove, also deal with salt air and ground moisture that speed up corrosion on fittings. None of this means a slab leak is inevitable, only that the floor is a hard place to spot trouble early.

Signs the leak is under the slab

Slab leaks hide, but they usually leave clues. Several of these together make a strong case.

Warm or hot spots on the floor

A patch of tile or concrete that feels warm when nothing underneath should produce heat often marks a leaking hot line.

The sound of running water

A faint hiss or trickle at night, with every tap off, suggests a pressurized line leaking under the floor.

Wet carpet, lifting tile or buckled boards

Moisture rising from below pushes up flooring, loosens grout and leaves a damp smell.

A bill or meter that does not add up

Use the meter test. With everything off, a moving dial points to a leak. Our leak detection team can confirm where.

New floor or wall cracks

A leak that saturates soil can shift the ground slightly and open hairline cracks, though cracks alone do not prove a leak.

Low hot water pressure

If showers lose force and the heater runs more than usual, a hidden loss on the hot side may be the reason.

There is more than one way to deal with a slab leak. The right one depends on how many leaks there are, where the pipe runs and what your floors are made of.

Honest trade-offs between spot repair, rerouting and repiping

A spot repair is the least invasive choice, and it is right when one clean leak sits on a line in good shape. The risk is that a pipe that failed once may fail again nearby, especially if it is the same age and material along its length.

Rerouting through the walls or ceiling avoids breaking concrete and gives you access for any future repair. It does mean cutting and patching drywall, and it only makes sense if the new route is clear. Full repiping is the bigger project, but it removes the buried line from the equation. It is worth discussing when you have had more than one leak.

If water burst through suddenly instead of seeping, our burst pipe repair service covers the emergency side. We would rather tell you a spot repair is enough than sell a bigger job you do not need.

Homeowners policies often cover the water damage that results from a sudden slab leak, and some cover the cost of tearing out to reach it. They usually do not pay to replace the worn pipe itself, and gradual leaks are often excluded. Check your policy and call your insurer before repairs begin.
