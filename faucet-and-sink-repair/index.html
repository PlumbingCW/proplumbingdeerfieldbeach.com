Most drips are cheap to fix, and a few are not

A faucet drip feels minor until you notice the bill or the stain in the basin. Inside the body, a washer, a ceramic disc or a cartridge controls the flow, and rubber seals and O-rings keep water from escaping around the handle. When one of those wears out, you get a drip at the spout or a slow weep around the base.

Replacing a cartridge is a sensible DIY job when you can shut off the supply and your faucet has a common design. It goes wrong when the stop valve will not close, when the handle seizes on its stem, or when corrosion locks the parts together. At that point, forcing it can snap a supply line behind the wall.

Salt air also plays a part for homes near Main Beach and The Cove. Chrome and brass fittings pit and crust faster in a coastal climate, so a faucet that looks fine may have a stuck stem underneath.

What to do when the faucet will not shut off or the cabinet is filling

A fixture that cannot be turned off is a flood in slow motion. Your goal in the first minutes is to isolate the water, not to fix the part.

Find the angle stop

Under a sink, each supply line has a small valve called an angle stop. Turn it clockwise until it stops. If it turns freely without slowing the flow, it has failed and you need the next valve upstream.

Close the main

If the stop is stuck or missing, shut the main water valve where the line enters the building. Open a low faucet afterward to bleed off pressure.

Empty and dry the cabinet

Remove cleaning products and boxes, and put a bowl under any active drip. Wet particleboard swells quickly, so move fast and wipe the floor of the cabinet.

Note where the water is coming from

Watch whether it is the faucet, a supply line, the trap or the sink rim. A quick photo helps us arrive with the right parts.

If water is running near an outlet, a garbage disposal switch or an appliance cord, keep clear until the power is off at the breaker. Tell us on the phone, and we will adjust how we approach the job.

Where faucets and sinks leak around a Florida home

Leaks hide in different places on different fixtures. This is the list we work through when someone describes a wet spot.

  • Kitchen and bathroom faucet bodies Drips at the spout or oozing at the base usually mean a worn cartridge or seal that we can replace.
  • Shower valve and showerhead Constant dripping from the head or a handle that will not stay off points to a bad cartridge inside the wall valve.
  • Bathtub spout and diverter Water from the spout while the shower runs means the diverter has worn, and a loose spout can leak behind the wall.
  • Outdoor spigots and hose bibs A hose bib that drips at the handle or sprays behind the hose needs new packing or a new valve, since the leak runs into the wall.
  • Laundry and utility sinks Faucets, supply lines and washing machine valves share a corner of the house, and any one of them may be the source.
  • Refrigerator water line The small valve behind a fridge or at the wall feeds the ice maker, and a drip there often goes unseen.
  • Sink drain, trap and basket strainer A wet bottom of the cabinet with a dry faucet points down at the trap, a slip nut or the drain flange.

A slow or standing drain at a sink is a different problem, and our drain cleaning team handles clogs in the trap and the line beyond.

Fix, swap or replace the whole faucet

Not every leak deserves a new faucet, and not every old faucet deserves another repair. This is how the choice usually plays out.

How we replace a faucet or valve

A replacement is straightforward when each step is done in order and the old connections are tested before the new ones go on.

  1. 1

    Isolate and drain

    We close the stops, open the faucet, and clear the cabinet so we can work without kneeling in water.

  2. 2

    Remove the old unit

    We disconnect the supply lines, loosen the mounting nuts and lift the faucet out, checking for corrosion on the sink deck.

  3. 3

    Prepare the connections

    We replace tired supply lines and stops, clean the mounting surface and dry-fit the new faucet.

  4. 4

    Install and seal

    The new faucet is set, tightened evenly and connected, with plumber's putty or a gasket where the base meets the sink.

  5. 5

    Flush and test

    We run both hot and cold, check each joint with a dry paper towel, and clear any debris from the aerator.

Price factors for faucet and sink work

A cartridge swap and a full faucet replacement with new stops are very different jobs. You get a clear price before work starts.

Fixture typeA single-handle bathroom faucet, a pull-down kitchen model and a shower valve each take different effort.
Access under or behind the fixtureA tight cabinet or a valve behind a tile wall takes longer than an open sink.
Condition of stops and supply linesReplacing corroded stops and braided lines adds parts and time.
Parts availabilityCommon cartridges are easy to source, while older or specialty faucets may need ordering.
Leak locationA surface drip differs from a leak behind a wall that needs opening and patching.

Homeowners policies often cover sudden water damage from a failed supply line or valve, but they usually do not pay for the worn faucet itself. Check your policy and photograph any damage before cleanup.

Slow drips that run for months are often treated as maintenance, not accidents, so fixing a leak early protects both your home and any later claim.
